Wix announced a round of layoffs yesterday. Webflow followed today. Two of the biggest names in the website-builder category cutting staff inside 48 hours is not a coincidence. It is a re-pricing.

The thing being re-priced is the moat. Both companies built their valuations on the assumption that once a customer learned their canvas, picked a template, wired up their forms and their CMS bindings and their SEO settings, moving to anything else was painful enough that they would not bother. That assumption is dying in public. AI agents do not learn a canvas. They translate between canvases. The hardest part of leaving — the part that kept revenue sticky for a decade — is becoming a prompt.

So the obvious question, the one I want to answer here: when moving between platforms costs nothing, who wins?

## What the website builders were actually selling

Wix and Webflow were never really selling hosting. Hosting is a commodity. They were selling four things, in roughly this order:

1. A learning curve you had already paid for.
2. A template and component ecosystem that would have taken weeks to recreate.
3. Glue code: forms, CRMs, payments, analytics, third-party integrations, all pre-wired.
4. Migration risk: redirects, SEO equity, broken links, the cost of getting it wrong.

Every one of those four is something an AI agent now does for you. The learning curve does not matter when the agent drives the UI. The template ecosystem does not matter when the agent can generate a custom design from a description in seconds. The glue code does not matter when the agent rewrites it for the new stack. And migration risk — the one that kept enterprise customers in place even when they hated the product — is the part that is collapsing fastest. I wrote about this from the SEO side in [SEO domain migrations](/seo-domain-migrations/) back when migrations were still a human craft. They are not, anymore.

What is left, once you strip those four away, is hosting and a render target. That is not a moat. That is a line item.

## The general rule when switching is free

When switching costs collapse, the value of a moat equals the value of what AI cannot copy. That is the whole framework. Everything else is application.

AI is very good at copying:

- Software UX patterns.
- Template ecosystems and design systems.
- Integration glue and migration scripts.
- Marketing copy, documentation, support answers.
- Anything that has been written down enough times for a model to learn it.

AI is not good at copying:

- Customer relationships and email lists. You have them or you do not.
- Brand and trust, built over years of shipping and not-shipping.
- Proprietary data — data nobody else has, ideally generated by your own customers using your own product.
- Real network effects, where each new participant makes the thing more valuable for the existing ones. Two-sided marketplaces. Social graphs. Payment networks.
- Physical-world operations. Warehouses. Delivery. Hardware. Sensors. Anything with atoms.
- Regulatory position. Licences, certifications, compliance, the cost of being allowed to exist.
- Distribution to humans. The browser homepage, the App Store front, the search results page.

Anything in the first list is becoming a commodity at speed. Anything in the second list is becoming more valuable, because the things around it got cheaper.

## Who wins, concretely

Three groups.

### The owners of the demand side

If your moat lives with the customer rather than inside your software, you win. A creator with an email list of 100,000 readers wins, because the list moves with them no matter what platform renders the newsletter. A B2B company with a real sales relationship wins, because the relationship was never in the CRM. A brand people trust wins, because trust took ten years to build and an AI cannot regenerate it from a prompt.

This is the same argument I have been making for a while in a different costume: own the audience, own the content, the [platform is replaceable](/do-you-need-a-cms/). I [argued this for agency](/open-source-agency/) and for the [machine-readable web](/standards-dont-prove-themselves/). It is the same point. The things you control directly are the things that survive a platform shift.

### The infrastructure underneath

Cloudflare, the hyperscalers, the model providers, the payment rails, the identity layer. These do not care which website builder the customer churns to next, because they are underneath all of them. Picks-and-shovels businesses always do well in a gold rush, but they do *especially* well in a gold rush where the prospectors keep switching mines every six weeks.

The cruel irony for Wix and Webflow is that they sit *just above* this layer. One floor down is Cloudflare adding "build me a site" as a free feature on top of Pages. One floor up is an AI agent that will render directly to any host. The website-builder floor is the floor getting squeezed from both sides.

### Whoever bet on portability before it was forced on them

The companies that built for the open web, for portable data, for "the customer can leave any time", have spent years carrying that cost. They look overbuilt in a world of lock-in. They look prescient in a world without it.

WordPress is the obvious case. It is messy and old and its market share is [under genuine pressure](/wordpress-market-share-shrinking/), but the data model is portable, the hosting is portable, the plugins are portable, the export is real. When switching is free, "we made switching easy on purpose" stops being a polite lie and starts being the product.

The same logic applies to anything built on open standards: markdown for content, JSON-LD for structure, RSS for syndication, MCP and similar protocols for agent access. None of these are moats. That is the point. They are the absence of moats, and the absence of moats is what an AI-driven world rewards.

## Who loses

Anyone whose moat was switching friction. Anyone whose pricing power came from "it would be a nightmare to leave." Anyone in the middle layer of SaaS without proprietary data or a real network effect underneath them.

The website-builder category is the most visible example this week, but it is not the only one. The same logic eats:

- CRMs whose differentiation was the integrations catalogue.
- Marketing automation tools whose differentiation was the template library.
- Analytics products whose differentiation was the dashboard UI.
- Helpdesk software whose differentiation was the workflow editor.

Every one of those will face the same conversation in the next eighteen months. Their customers will run the numbers, talk to an agent, and discover that the migration they had been putting off for three years now takes a weekend.

## What this means if you are building

Stop investing in friction. Friction is not a defensible asset anymore, even if it shows up beautifully in your retention curves. Invest in the things that survive the migration: the audience, the data, the brand, the network, the relationship.

If you are running a business *on* one of these platforms rather than building one, the same rule inverts in your favour. The leverage you have over your tools just went up by a large multiple. Use it. Own your domain. Own your content as plain files. Own your list. Treat every platform — including the one you are happy with today — as a render target you could swap out in a week. Because you can.

The companies cutting staff this week are not the last ones who will have to. They are the first ones who could not pretend any longer.
